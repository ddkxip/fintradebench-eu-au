\title{Categorical, Not Distributional: How Multi-Agent Financial Reasoners
Express Uncertainty}

\author{Anonymous Author(s)}
\affiliation{%

  \country{}

\begin{abstract}
Multi-agent debate is increasingly proposed as a way to make large language
model (LLM) reasoning more reliable, and uncertainty quantification (UQ) is
the natural instrument for checking whether it works. We study both on
gold-anchored financial reasoning questions and find that the standard UQ
lens is mismatched to how these systems actually behave. Rather than
spreading probability mass across answers, multi-agent financial reasoners
converge---with high self-consistency---on \emph{non-committal labels}
(\texttt{mixed}, \texttt{conditional}, \texttt{insufficient\_data}). We
quantify this by pairing an exact total\,=\,aleatoric\,+\,epistemic entropy
decomposition over schema-constrained answer spaces with a first-class
non-commitment mass, $p_{\mathrm{nc}}$, and evaluating 139 expert-labelled
questions across four open-weights models plus an API model. Four results
follow. (i) The dominant failure mode is a \emph{hedge collision}---the
system declines to commit while the reference answer is directional---at
$50.6\%$--$67.0\%$ of all errors. (ii) $p_{\mathrm{nc}}$ predicts error
(AUROC $0.64$--$0.72$), but decomposition shows this is a sign-flipping
interaction rather than a general uncertainty signal: its slope reverses on
questions whose reference answer is itself non-committal. (iii) Whether a
system hedges is a model$\times$question-type property, not a capability
level: the cross-signal lane is the highest-hedging lane in all five models
tested, including ones that commit confidently elsewhere. (iv) Controlled
evidence interventions---removing the single most important expert
indicator, injecting cross-signal conflict, dialling the horizon---fail to
move the uncertainty channels, and injected conflict \emph{lowers} measured
disagreement as both agents retreat to a shared hedge. We further show, on a
second benchmark, that the apparent absence of a directional error signal in
$p_{\mathrm{nc}}$ is a degeneracy artifact that disappears when residual
non-commitment mass has variance. Together these results argue that
uncertainty in deployed multi-agent reasoners should be measured
categorically, and that entropy-style decompositions can be uninformative
precisely where such systems fail.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{large language models, multi-agent debate, uncertainty
quantification, financial reasoning, evaluation}

\section{Introduction}

A now-standard recipe for improving LLM reliability is to have several model
instances argue: multi-agent debate. A parallel literature quantifies model
uncertainty, typically by treating the model as a distribution over answers
and measuring entropy or its decomposition into epistemic and aleatoric
parts. Combining the two is attractive---uncertainty should tell us when
debate helps, and debate should reduce the reducible part of uncertainty.

We test that combination in a domain where it matters and where reference
answers exist: financial reasoning over company fundamentals and trading
signals. Our starting instrument is the standard one. For a question $q$
with a finite answer space $\mathcal{Y}$, $N$ agents produce label
distributions $p_i$ estimated by self-consistency; the system mixture is
$\bar p = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i p_i$ and

\begin{equation}
\underbrace{H(\bar p)}_{\mathrm{TU}} =
\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{N}\textstyle\sum_i H(p_i)}_{\mathrm{AU}} +
\underbrace{\mathrm{TU}-\mathrm{AU}}_{\mathrm{EU}},
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}

where EU is the generalized Jensen--Shannon divergence among agents.

The instrument turns out to be poorly matched to the phenomenon. In our
runs, the systems are frequently \emph{near-deterministic}: up to
$77\%$ of question--model cells have $\mathrm{TU}=0$ exactly, meaning every
decode of every agent produced the same label. What varies is not how
probability is spread but \emph{which} label the system settles on---and
very often that label is a non-committal one. We therefore promote the mass
a system places on non-committal labels,
\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{nc}}(q) = \sum_{y \in \mathcal{N}(q)} \bar p(y),
\end{equation}
with $\mathcal{N}(q)$ the question's non-committal labels, to a first-class
quantity alongside \eqref{eq:decomp}.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A gold-anchored evaluation of categorical versus distributional
uncertainty in multi-agent financial reasoning: 139 expert-labelled
questions, canonical answer schemas, four open-weights models (plus an API
model), self-consistency estimation with $K{=}10$--$20$.
\item The \emph{hedge collision} result: non-commitment against a
directional reference answer is the modal error type in every model, and the
error structure is question-type dependent (fundamental/hybrid lanes fail by
hedging; the trading lane fails by committing to the wrong entity).
\item A precise account of what $p_{\mathrm{nc}}$ does and does not detect,
including a sign-flipping interaction and---via a second benchmark---the
degeneracy condition under which its apparent limitation disappears.
\item A controlled intervention suite showing that the uncertainty channels
are not evidence-responsive in the hedging regime, including the result that
injected cross-signal conflict \emph{reduces} measured epistemic
uncertainty.
\item An independent blinded re-annotation of the commitment labels
(Cohen's $\kappa=0.59$) demonstrating that the conclusions do not depend on
contestable labelling decisions.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Multi-agent debate.} Debate among LLM instances has been proposed
to improve factuality and reasoning~\cite{du2023debate}. Recent work
questions when it helps, showing that debate dynamics are largely fixed in
early rounds and that homogeneous panels can fail to improve expected
correctness. Work on consensus pathologies documents false consensus and
conformity, including counterfactual comparisons against no-communication
controls~\cite{cagecal2026,flips2026}. Our transitions taxonomy deliberately
mirrors those categories so that our rates are comparable; our contribution
is their \emph{domain- and source-conditional} structure, and the finding
that in the hedging regime consensus forms \emph{before} debate.

\paragraph{Uncertainty quantification for LLMs.} Semantic
entropy~\cite{kuhn2023semantic,farquhar2024detecting} and
self-consistency~\cite{wang2023selfconsistency} estimate uncertainty by
sampling; calibration of verbalized confidence is known to be
weak~\cite{xiong2024canllms}; ensemble decompositions separate epistemic
from aleatoric components~\cite{depeweg2018decomposition}. Closest to us,
recent work decomposes debate uncertainty into epistemic gain and aleatoric
cost on mathematical reasoning~\cite{egac2026}, and source-conditioned
evaluation shows UQ methods degrade away from the uncertainty source they
were designed for~\cite{whydontyouknow2026}. We differ in domain, in
anchoring every quantity to expert reference answers, and---centrally---in
showing that in this setting the informative variance is categorical rather
than distributional.

\paragraph{Financial LLM agents.} Multi-agent systems for finance are
typically evaluated on returns or task accuracy~\cite{tradingagents2024,
findebate2025}. We are not proposing a system: we treat an intentionally
simple two-analyst debate as an object of measurement, and ask what its
uncertainty signals mean.

\section{Setup}

\subsection{Benchmark and answer schemas}

We use a financial reasoning benchmark of expert-audited questions over
NASDAQ-100 companies with three question types (``lanes''): fundamentals
(F), trading signals (T), and hybrid cross-signal reasoning (FT). Each
question ships with a gold long-form response and an expert-specified set of
required indicators.

Distribution-level scoring requires a finite answer space, so we construct a
canonical \textsc{AnswerSchema} per question: an answer type, an exhaustive
and mutually exclusive label set, a gold label, and a verbatim quote from
the gold response supporting that label. Of 150 questions, 139 reduce
cleanly; 11 are excluded (unordered multi-entity screening answers and one
degenerate gold). Non-commitment is a first-class outcome: the gold label is
itself non-committal on $15.8\%$ ($22/139$) of questions, so hedging is
sometimes the \emph{correct} answer, not a failure.

\subsection{Agents, evidence, and estimation}

Two agents---a Fundamental analyst and a Trading-Signal analyst---answer
each question from an \emph{oracle evidence pack} reconstructed with the
benchmark's own context builder (windowed price/indicator values and
period-median fundamentals). Retrieval is deliberately excluded from the
headline condition so that debate is measured conditional on fixed evidence.
Agents never see the gold response.

Following a reliability study on this stack, agent distributions are
estimated by \emph{self-consistency frequencies} over $K$ decodes at
$\tau{=}0.7$ rather than verbalized probabilities, which we found heavily
heaped (70\% of emitted values on a $0.05$ grid) and barely more informative
than replicate noise. We use $K{=}10$ (F, FT) and $K{=}20$ (T, which is
noisier), apply a Miller--Madow correction to plug-in entropies, and report
per-lane results rather than pooling naively.

Round~0 is independent; in round~1 each agent sees the opponent's modal
label and rationale and may revise. Metrics are computed at both rounds.

\subsection{Error taxonomy}

Given a gold label $y^\ast$ and system prediction $\hat y$, and writing
$\mathcal{N}$ for the non-committal labels, each scored item falls into one
cell of a $2\times2$ commitment grid. Errors partition into: \emph{hedge
collision} ($y^\ast \notin \mathcal{N}$, $\hat y \in \mathcal{N}$);
\emph{wrong-direction commitment} (both committed, $\hat y \neq y^\ast$);
\emph{overcommitment} ($y^\ast \in \mathcal{N}$, $\hat y \notin
\mathcal{N}$); and \emph{wrong non-commitment type} (both non-committal but
different). Two cells are deterministic by construction---a non-committal
prediction cannot match a committed gold---so the \emph{committed-gold and
committed-prediction} cell is the one in which $p_{\mathrm{nc}}$ is not
definitionally tied to the outcome. We rely on it throughout as the
non-mechanical test.

\section{Experiments}

We report four open-weights models at full scale (139 questions, both
rounds): two small (\textsc{gemma4}, \textsc{qwen3:8b}) and two mid-size
(\textsc{qwen3.6-27b}, \textsc{gemma4-31b-it}), plus a 16-question subset on
an API model (\textsc{gemini-3.1-pro}) reported separately and never pooled.
Parse rates are $99.8\%$ or above. Beyond the natural runs we conduct: a
controlled intervention suite (evidence removal with a matched placebo,
cross-signal conflict injection, horizon dialling); an independent blinded
re-annotation of gold commitment; and an auxiliary probe on a second
benchmark.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Uncertainty is categorical, and errors are hedge collisions}

Table~\ref{tab:decomp} gives the error decomposition. Hedge collision is the
modal error type in \emph{every} model, at $50.6\%$--$67.0\%$ of errors
(pooled $57.2\%$). The structure is strongly lane-dependent: pooled across
models, $71\%$ of F-lane and $76\%$ of FT-lane errors are hedge collisions,
whereas $77\%$ of T-lane errors are wrong-direction commitments. The two
lanes fail in different ways, and only one of those ways is visible to a
non-commitment signal.

Hedging is not intrinsically wrong. On the $22$ questions whose gold is
itself non-committal, a hedging system is correct $57\%$--$93\%$ of the
time. The pathology is hedging on the $84\%$ of questions whose reference
answer is directional.

\subsection{What $p_{\mathrm{nc}}$ actually detects}

$p_{\mathrm{nc}}$ predicts error well in aggregate (AUROC $0.636$--$0.721$
across the four models) and survives controls: in a logistic model with
total uncertainty, lane, and model, clustered by primary ticker, its
coefficient is significant under Benjamini--Hochberg correction. But a
single-slope model is misspecified. Adding an interaction with whether the
gold is itself non-committal (Table~\ref{tab:interaction}) reverses the
slope---$+5.12$ on committed-gold questions versus $-8.90$ on
non-committal-gold questions---and raises pseudo-$R^2$ from $0.152$ to
$0.388$ ($n{=}556$; interaction $p=1.7\times10^{-10}$).

Critically, much of the aggregate signal is \emph{mechanical}: a
non-committal prediction cannot match a committed gold. In the
non-mechanical cell (committed gold \emph{and} committed prediction),
AUROC falls to $0.403$--$0.446$ in all four models---at or below chance.
On these data, conditional on the system taking a position, the residual
hedge mass says nothing about whether the position is right. Section~\ref{sec:boundary}
shows this conclusion is regime-dependent, not a property of the signal.

\subsection{Regimes are model$\times$lane, not capability levels}

A natural hypothesis is that hedging is a small-model pathology cured by
capability. The data reject the simple version. Table~\ref{tab:lane} shows
that the FT (cross-signal) lane has the highest non-commitment mass in
\emph{all five} models. The two mid-size models commit confidently on F
(accuracy $0.55$--$0.61$, the best F-lane results we observe, exceeding the
API model) while simultaneously hedging on FT ($p_{\mathrm{nc}}=0.73$
and $0.74$). Capability buys commitment on single-signal questions and does
not buy it where fundamentals and price action must be reconciled---the
same condition our conflict intervention manipulates directly
(\S\ref{sec:interventions}).

\subsection{Debate reduces instability only where instability exists}

In three of four models, one debate round significantly reduces AU
($-0.070$ to $-0.149$; Wilcoxon $p<10^{-6}$) while leaving EU flat. The
fourth is instructive: \textsc{gemma4-31b-it} shows no AU reduction
($-0.005$, n.s.) and instead a \emph{significant EU increase}
($+0.036$, $p=0.035$). Its agents are already near-deterministic (final-round
AU $0.012$; $77\%$ of cells with $\mathrm{TU}=0$), so there is no
within-agent instability left to remove and debate instead pushes the
frameworks apart. We therefore state the regularity with its scope
condition: \emph{debate reduces within-agent instability where such
instability exists; where agents are already near-deterministic it increases
between-agent divergence instead.}

Two further debate results matter for practice. First, false consensus is
real but modest ($1.4\%$--$7.9\%$ of questions) and, in the most hedging
models, structurally pre-empted---consensus forms \emph{before} debate,
on non-committal labels. Second, and more consequential: \emph{no} round-0
uncertainty quantity, and no uncertainty delta, predicts whether debate will
improve the gold-label probability (all $p>0.34$). Uncertainty cannot be
used to route debate on these data.

\subsection{Uncertainty channels are not evidence-responsive}
\label{sec:interventions}

We ran randomized, paired interventions against the stored oracle baseline
($30$ F/FT questions): removing the highest-priority \emph{present} expert
indicator; a matched placebo removing an equally-sized non-expert indicator;
coherently rewriting the trading channel to oppose the fundamental lean; and
dialling the stated horizon between three months and five years with
evidence fixed.

None of the pre-registered on-target predictions is confirmed. Removing the
most important indicator produces no selective movement in any channel, and
the diagonal-versus-placebo contrast is null on every metric---the
uncertainty does not track \emph{which} evidence is present. Dialling the
horizon flips $3/30$ verdicts. Most informative is conflict injection:
prediction was that explicit cross-signal contradiction would raise EU;
instead EU \emph{falls} ($-0.041$, bootstrap CI excluding $0$) and the
gold-label probability drops, because both agents retreat to the \emph{same}
non-committal label and therefore agree \emph{more}. The debate looks most
consensual exactly when the evidence is most contradictory.

We report these nulls as a boundary on construct validity: in the hedging
regime, the entropy decomposition is a stable stylistic property of the
system rather than an evidence-driven measurement.

\subsection{The commitment labels are not the load-bearing assumption}

Because our taxonomy rests on classifying each gold as committed or not, we
had two independent annotators re-adjudicate all 139 golds from a
\emph{blinded} sheet (question, answer space, and the gold's own text; no
schema label, no prior classification). They agreed with each other on
$86.3\%$ of commitment calls (Cohen's $\kappa = 0.590$; Fleiss $\kappa =
0.531$ over three annotators) and disagreed with the schema in opposite
directions---one reading two-dimension ``mixed'' golds as committed, the
other reading multi-entity screening golds as non-committal. Under every
annotator's labels, the majority vote, and a strict blinded consensus,
hedge collision remains the modal error type ($0.468$--$0.670$) and the
non-mechanical cell remains at chance. Moderate $\kappa$ is honest---the
judgement is genuinely borderline at the margin---but the conclusions are
invariant to it.

\subsection{A generalization boundary}
\label{sec:boundary}

An auxiliary probe on a second benchmark (37 yes/no questions answered from
human-annotated oracle evidence, same \textsc{qwen3:8b} model) replicates the
central phenomenon even more strongly: hedge collision accounts for $79.2\%$
of errors and aggregate AUROC reaches $0.955$. But it \emph{overturns} the
``no directional signal'' reading of \S5.2: there, the non-mechanical cell
gives AUROC $0.815$ (bootstrap $95\%$ CI $[0.631, 0.958]$, excluding
chance).

The mechanism is variance, not mean. In the main benchmark, committed
predictions carried almost no residual hedge mass (e.g.\ mean $0.079$, sd
$0.174$), so there was nothing to rank and chance-level AUROC was
mechanically forced. In the probe, systems often commit while retaining
substantial hedge mass (mean $0.247$, sd $0.255$), and wrong answers carry
roughly three times the hedge mass of right ones. The correct claim is
therefore scoped: \emph{$p_{\mathrm{nc}}$ carries wrong-direction signal
only when residual non-commitment mass has meaningful variance}; in the
near-degenerate regimes typical of small open-weights models it does not,
which is why it can appear to be a pure hedge-collision detector.

\section{Discussion}

Our results suggest a reframing for anyone measuring uncertainty in deployed
multi-agent reasoners. The quantity that predicts failure here is not how
uncertain the system \emph{is} but \emph{what it says when it is uncertain}.
Because non-commitment is a discrete lexical choice, a system can be
maximally self-consistent (zero entropy) and maximally uninformative at the
same time---a state that entropy-based UQ scores as confident.

This has three practical consequences. First, monitoring non-commitment mass
is a cheap and effective triage signal wherever reference answers are mostly
directional---but it is a hedge-collision detector, not a general error
predictor, and it is blind to the wrong-entity errors that dominate the
trading lane. Second, the routing intuition---spend more compute where
uncertainty is high---is unsupported here: nothing in the uncertainty
profile predicts whether debate will help. Third, benchmark designers should
treat non-commitment as a scored outcome with its own gold labels, as it is
sometimes the correct answer; otherwise systems are penalized for
appropriate caution and rewarded for overconfident guessing.

\section{Limitations}

Reference answers are ex-post benchmark labels, not market truth;
correctness means agreement with an expert-audited conclusion given the same
evidence. Commitment labelling is moderately reliable ($\kappa \approx
0.53$--$0.59$), and we report invariance to it rather than claiming
objectivity. Pooled rows are not independent (each question appears once per
model), so per-model results are primary and regressions cluster by ticker.
EU is computed over $N{=}2$ framework agents---a single edge, not a graph.
The intervention suite ran on one model at $n{=}30$ and its causal nulls are
scoped to the hedging regime. The second-benchmark probe is small ($n{=}37$,
one model) and its debate arm used an asymmetric agent pair; a
neutral-agent control is underway and any cross-benchmark debate claim
should wait for it.

\section{Conclusion}

Multi-agent financial reasoners express uncertainty categorically rather
than distributionally. Measured against expert reference answers, their
dominant failure is declining to commit when the reference answer is
directional; whether they hedge depends on the question type as much as on
model capability; debate reduces within-agent instability only where such
instability exists and cannot be routed by uncertainty; and controlled
evidence interventions leave the uncertainty channels unmoved, including a
conflict manipulation that \emph{reduces} apparent disagreement. We release
the answer schemas, intervention suite, and per-question decompositions to
support replication.
